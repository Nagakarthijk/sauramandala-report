A five-year runway into Indian public life

पंचायत से पार्लियामेंट तकWe pay the rent. You do the politics.

Neev pays 500 organisers, activists and political workers a monthly living stipend, straight into their own bank account, for up to five years. It's for the early years of political life, when the work is real and the pay is zero. Win an election or take a paid party post and the stipend ends, because the job is done. Any party or none.

Why this exists

Politics starts long before anyone gets a seat. Nobody pays for those years.

Every MLA and MP was once someone organising a village meeting, a protest or a union with no salary. That unpaid decade decides who India's leaders are. The people who get through it mostly have family money, a party paying them as a wholetimer, or someone with an interest in their future.

Money is not the only reason the ladder breaks. Caste networks, party gatekeeping and plain violence all matter. But money is the one thing an outside fund can actually fix, and it decides who is still doing the work when a movement, a ticket or a party post opens up.

Panchayat reservation rotates. A woman sarpanch who did good work for five years often loses the seat when it is de-reserved, and then she has no income and no reason to keep showing up. Neev is built for that moment, and for the organiser who has done unpaid work for the union or the sangathan for eight years and is about to take a private job.

The stipend

Living costs, paid to you, every month.

The money is for rent, food, school fees and a bike that works. It goes into the fellow's own bank account. It doesn't go through an NGO, a party or a sangathan, and nobody takes a cut. It is not campaign money and never will be. The amount depends on how big an area your work covers, because organising a state costs more in travel and time than organising a village.

What else fellows get

A small budget for interns and volunteers in their area, a yearly gathering with the full cohort across parties, and access to people who can help with the unglamorous parts: RTI, budgets, reading a DPR, handling the press, legal aid when cases get filed against them.

What fellows don't have to do

No logframes. No monthly reports. No outcome targets. One honest conversation with us every quarter about how things are going. The money lands on the first of every month by bank transfer. If we can't run this with less paperwork than a government scheme, we've failed.

When it ends

Getting elected is graduating.

Neev funds the gap before a public salary. Once politics starts paying you, the stipend stops. We'd count that as a success, not a loss.

You win an election

Any elected office, from ward member to MP. The stipend stops the month you take office. Contesting is fine, and the stipend continues through a campaign, but it can never be spent on one.

You take a paid or official party post

Becoming an office-bearer or a paid wholetimer of any party ends it. Being an ordinary party member or volunteer does not.

Five years pass

Five years is the most anyone gets. That's long enough to build a base and short enough that people plan for what comes next. Most fellows will leave earlier, for office, a party post or other work.

You stop doing the work

If you move on from public work, tell us and the stipend winds down over three months. Nobody has to pay anything back.

People who leave become Neev alumni. That includes the ones who win. The alumni network is where an MLA who started as a fellow meets the organiser from a different party who started in the same cohort.

Where we look

We start with a map, not an application form.

Most fellowships wait for applications, which means they fund people who already know how to apply for fellowships. We work out where representation is thinnest first, then go looking for the people already doing the work there.

Who lives there

Census and other public data on the share of women, Dalit, Adivasi, OBC, Muslim and other minority residents in each panchayat, block, assembly and parliamentary constituency.

Who holds power there

Three or more election cycles of results and affidavits. Places where seats pass between the same few families are places where independent organising matters most.

Where reservation has just rotated

Panchayat seats that just came off reservation, where a capable representative has lost her seat and her honorarium but not her following.

Who is already organising

We ask people we trust on the ground, across parties and movements, who is doing real work in that area right now.

The point of the map is to spread 500 people sensibly: across states, levels, communities and parties. It is not a ranking of who deserves what.

Selection

Very selective, and slow on purpose.

Five years is a long commitment on both sides. We expect to say no far more often than yes.

  1. Nomination or applicationMost fellows will be nominated by people who have watched them work. Anyone can also apply directly, in any Indian language, on paper or by voice note.
  2. Field visitSomeone from Neev spends time in the area. We talk to the person's supporters and also to people who disagree with them.
  3. Track record checkCriminal cases, affidavits, public statements. A case filed against an organiser is not a disqualification on its own, since many are political. We read the cases.
  4. Cross-party panelA panel with people from different parties and from outside party politics makes the final call. No single person can select or remove a fellow.
  5. A first yearThe first year is a trial on both sides. After that the stipend runs until the fellow graduates, five years pass, or a hard line below is crossed.
Rules

What keeps this honest.

A fund that pays people in politics has to be stricter about itself than about the people it funds. These are the rules we're proposing.

Indian money only

Fellows do political work, and the Foreign Contribution (Regulation) Act restricts foreign money for political activity. Foreign money would also put every fellow at risk. Neev is funded only by Indian donors. No exceptions.

No campaign money

Stipends cover living costs. Neev never pays for posters, rallies, vehicles, ad spend or anything that counts as election expenditure, and never funds a sitting representative.

No party gets a majority

Fellows who lean towards a party are welcome, but no single party's people can make up more than a set share of the cohort. Plenty of fellows will have no party at all.

Where it stops

The stipend ends for inciting violence, hate speech against any community, or a corruption finding. A panel decides, with the fellow heard first.

Fellows can say they're funded

Fellows are encouraged to say publicly that they get the stipend, and to declare it as income if they contest. We publish the list of fellows and the list of donors every year.

Donors don't pick fellows

Donors give to the pool. They don't sponsor individuals, don't sit on selection panels and can't ask for anyone to be removed.

What it costs

Roughly ₹165 crore over five years.

That's the default below: 500 fellows on the tiered stipend for the full five years, a 5% yearly raise, and 20% on top for selection, field visits, the yearly gathering, intern budgets and running the place. Questions

Things people ask.

Isn't this just funding politicians?

No. Nobody holding an elected seat or a paid party post can be a fellow. Neev funds the organising, activism and political work that comes before any of that, and that many people do their whole lives without ever contesting.

Why does the money go to individuals and not organisations?

Because people leave organisations, organisations split, and a sangathan's funding decides whose work counts. A stipend paid to the person stays with them through all of that.

What if I contest and lose?

The stipend carries on. Losing is normal, and a loss shouldn't end someone's public life.

Can I be a member of a party?

Yes, as an ordinary member or volunteer, and you can switch parties. We fund the person, not the party. The stipend ends if you become an office-bearer or a paid party worker.

Can I keep my job or farm?

Yes. The stipend is meant to make public work possible, not to replace everything else in your life.

How do you know it worked?

At the end of five years we'll count how many fellows are still in public work, how many graduated into office or party roles, and who they are compared with the people who held those roles before. We'll publish the result even if it's bad.
