A ten-year experiment in Indian public life

पंचायत से पार्लियामेंट तकWe pay the rent. You do the politics.

Neev gives 500 people a monthly living stipend for ten years, so they can stay in public work without a rich family, a party treasurer or a contractor behind them. Any party or none. Any level, from the gram sabha to the Lok Sabha.

Why this exists

The ladder breaks somewhere between the panchayat and Parliament.

Reservation got women and Dalit and Adivasi leaders into panchayats in large numbers. Very few of them climb. Doing politics in India is a full-time job with no salary for the first decade, and the people who survive that decade are mostly the ones who can afford to.

Money is not the only reason the ladder breaks. Caste networks, party gatekeeping and plain violence all matter. But money is the one thing an outside fund can actually fix, and it decides who is even still around when a ticket, a post or a movement opens up.

Panchayat reservation rotates. A woman sarpanch who did good work for five years often loses the seat because it is de-reserved, and then she has no income and no reason to keep showing up. Neev is built for that moment, and for the organiser who has been doing unpaid work for the union or the sangathan for eight years and is about to take a private job.

The stipend

Living costs, every month, for ten years.

The money is for rent, food, school fees and a bike that works. It is not campaign money and it never will be. Fellows keep it whether they win, lose, switch parties or decide not to contest at all.

What else fellows get

A small budget for interns and volunteers in their area, a yearly gathering with the full cohort across parties, and access to people who can help with the unglamorous parts: RTI, budgets, reading a DPR, handling the press, legal aid when cases get filed against them.

What fellows don't have to do

No logframes. No monthly reports. No outcome targets. One honest conversation with us every quarter about how things are going. The money lands on the first of every month by bank transfer. If we can't run this with less paperwork than a government scheme, we've failed.

Where we look

We start with a map, not an application form.

Most fellowships wait for applications, which means they fund people who already know how to apply for fellowships. We work out where representation is thinnest first, then go looking for the people already doing the work there.

Who lives there

Census and other public data on the share of women, Dalit, Adivasi, OBC, Muslim and other minority residents in each panchayat, block, assembly and parliamentary constituency.

Who has held the seat

Three or more election cycles of results and affidavits. Seats that pass between the same few families get a flag.

Where reservation is about to rotate

Panchayat seats coming off reservation in the next cycle, where a capable representative is about to lose her platform.

Who is already organising

We ask people we trust on the ground, across parties and movements, who is doing real work in that area right now.

The point of the map is to spread 500 people sensibly: across states, levels, communities and parties. It is not a ranking of who deserves what.

Selection

Very selective, and slow on purpose.

Ten years is a long commitment on both sides. We expect to say no far more often than yes.

  1. Nomination or applicationMost fellows will be nominated by people who have watched them work. Anyone can also apply directly, in any Indian language, on paper or by voice note.
  2. Field visitSomeone from Neev spends time in the area. We talk to the person's supporters and also to people who disagree with them.
  3. Track record checkCriminal cases, affidavits, public statements. A case filed against an organiser is not a disqualification on its own, since many are political. We read the cases.
  4. Cross-party panelA panel with people from different parties and from outside party politics makes the final call. No single person can select or remove a fellow.
  5. A first yearThe first year is a trial on both sides. After that the stipend runs for the full ten years unless a hard line below is crossed.
Rules

What keeps this honest.

A fund that pays people in politics has to be stricter about itself than about the people it funds. These are the rules we're proposing.

Indian money only

The Foreign Contribution (Regulation) Act bars candidates, legislators and party office-bearers from taking foreign contributions. Neev will be funded by Indian donors through an Indian entity. No exceptions.

No campaign money

Stipends cover living costs. Neev never pays for posters, rallies, vehicles, ad spend or anything that counts as election expenditure.

No party gets a majority

No single party can make up more than a set share of the cohort. Fellows with no party affiliation are welcome at every level.

Where it stops

The stipend ends for inciting violence, hate speech against any community, or a corruption finding. A panel decides, with the fellow heard first.

Fellows can say they're funded

Fellows are encouraged to declare the stipend publicly and in affidavits as income. We publish the list of fellows and the list of donors every year.

Donors don't pick fellows

Donors give to the pool. They don't sponsor individuals, don't sit on selection panels and can't ask for anyone to be removed.

What it costs

Roughly ₹350–400 crore over ten years.

That's the default below: 500 fellows on the tiered stipend, a 5% yearly raise, and 20% on top for selection, field visits, the yearly gathering, intern budgets and running the place. Change any number and the total updates.

Questions

Things people ask.

Isn't this just funding politicians?

It funds people to stay in public life long enough to be useful. Most of them will be at the panchayat and block level, where a sarpanch's honorarium can be a few thousand rupees a month. Some will never contest anything.

What if a fellow loses an election?

Nothing changes. Losing is normal. The stipend exists so that a loss doesn't end someone's public life.

What if a fellow switches parties?

Nothing changes. We fund the person, not the party.

Can I keep my job or farm?

Yes. The stipend is meant to make public work possible, not to replace everything else in your life.

Can sitting MLAs and MPs be fellows?

Yes, if they need it. We expect most fellows at those levels to be people preparing to contest rather than people already holding the seat.

How do you know it worked?

After ten years we'll count how many fellows are still in public life, at what level, and how the cohort compares with who held those seats before. We'll publish the result even if it's bad.
